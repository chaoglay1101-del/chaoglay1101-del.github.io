Research

Digital payment pressure and bank net fee margins

Thesis title: The Impact of Digital Payment Pressure on Banks' Fee-Based Income: Evidence from ASEAN Banking Markets

Evidence from an ASEAN-3 bank panel, 2014–2022

This page is the readable version of my undergraduate research. It states the question, how the panel was built, what the fixed-effects models show, and — at the same length — what the design cannot tell you.

Question

Why the direction of the relationship is not obvious

Is digital payment pressure associated with bank net fee margins in Indonesia, Malaysia, and Thailand?

The question grew out of my Business Analytics Tableau project (December 2025). The study does not assume that digital payments help or hurt banks. Digitalisation can affect fee income through several channels at once, and they push in different directions:

Channel 1

Transaction and service expansion

Digital channels lower friction, increase usage, and can support new payment or merchant services.

Channel 2

Competitive pressure

FinTech firms, payment networks, and digital-first competitors may compress prices or reallocate payment-related revenue.

Channel 3

Customer relationships and cross-selling

Frequent digital engagement and transaction data can create opportunities in deposits, lending, insurance, or wealth management.

Channel 4

Institutional and market differences

Payment infrastructure, regulation, financial inclusion, and bank business models differ substantially across countries.

Two competing hypotheses, and two refinements

Hypothesis Theory Prediction Result
H1a Substitution Contestable markets (Baumol, 1982); liquidity adjustment costs (Verdier, 2024) β₁ < 0 Consistent: negative and significant at 5% in the pooled log model
H1b Complementarity Delegated monitoring (Diamond, 1984); bank–FinTech coexistence (Thakor, 2020); API and B2B alliances (Brandl and Hornuf, 2020) β₁ > 0 Not supported
H2 Scale heterogeneity Scale economies in technology (Berger, 2003) Larger banks more resilient Not supported: interaction −0.003930 (n.s.)
H3 Non-linear transition Substitution first, complementarity as markets mature U-shape, β₂ > 0 Not supported: squared term −0.001988 (n.s.)

H1a and H1b need not be mutually exclusive: competitive pressure may dominate early, while intermediation and platform channels strengthen as infrastructure matures, which is what H3 tests.

Because these channels can offset each other, a negative coefficient would not by itself show that digital payments reduce bank income. It could reflect market composition, country-specific institutions, or omitted factors that move together with both payment activity and bank outcomes.

Data

Two data levels joined into one panel

Outcome and controls

Bank-year variables

  • Net_Fee_Margin — net fee and commission income relative to total assets, proxied by Compustat Global total non-interest income — the closest series available consistently across the three markets, which also contains trading and other non-interest revenue.
  • Size_USD — natural log of total assets converted into US dollars.
  • Capital_Adequacy_Proxy — equity-to-assets ratio (EQRAT in the thesis): book equity / total assets.
  • Credit_Risk — loan loss provisions / total assets.
  • Interest_Expense_Ratio — interest expense / total assets.
  • Deposit_Ratio — total deposits / total assets.
Explanatory measure

Country-year digital payment pressure

The explanatory variable comes from the IMF Financial Access Survey indicator IMF_FAS_FCMIBT, used with the unit PT_GDP: mobile and internet banking transactions as a percentage of GDP. It counts transactions processed by banks themselves, not by non-bank FinTech platforms. Between 2014 and 2022 it rose from 111% to 268% of GDP in Indonesia, from 366% to 719% in Malaysia and from 124% to 532% in Thailand. In the linear models one unit equals 10 percentage points of GDP (DPP_10pct_GDP); the refined models use ln(1 + DPP).

  • The indicator source is public; generated extraction files are not committed to the repository.
  • Availability, definitions, and reuse terms can change, so the workflow documents how the indicator was discovered and selected instead of treating it as a black box.

Method

Fixed effects, clustered inference, and explicit cleaning rules

The workflow separates panel preparation, indicator extraction, merging, estimation, and robustness checks, so each decision can be inspected on its own.

Specification

NFMit = β DPPct + γ′Xit + αi + λt + εit
  • NFM is bank i's net fee margin in year t.
  • DPP is the digital-payment-pressure measure for country c in year t.
  • X is a vector of bank-level controls.
  • α is a bank fixed effect and λ is a year fixed effect, which absorb persistent bank differences and common time shocks.
  • The coefficient β is a conditional association inside the stated model, not an automatically causal effect.
Estimation choices

How the models were run

  • A complete-case sample is formed on the required outcome, explanatory variable, and controls so specifications are comparable.
  • Continuous variables are winsorised at the 1st and 99th percentiles within the analysis sample.
  • Standard errors are clustered at the bank level to account for dependence within bank histories. Driscoll–Kraay standard errors, robust to cross-sectional dependence, are reported alongside.
  • Country subsamples keep bank fixed effects and drop year effects, because a country-year explanatory variable would otherwise be absorbed in a single-country model.
Specifications tested

From baseline to robustness

  • Scaled digital-payment pressure (DPP_10pct_GDP).
  • Scaled pressure with additional financial controls, then a full-controls specification.
  • A logarithmic baseline (Log_Digital_Payment_Pressure).
  • A bank-size interaction (Digital_x_LargeBank).
  • A centred nonlinear specification with a squared log term.
  • Country-subsample estimates of the log specification.
  • Robustness: Driscoll–Kraay standard errors, a Mundlak between/within decomposition, excluding 2020, a net-interest-margin placebo, a one-year lag, and excluding Islamic and digital-challenger banks.

How to read this table

  • The scaled and log models use different units, so the coefficient sizes are not comparable across rows; only the sign and significance are.
  • The pooled log association is statistically significant, but country subsamples are heterogeneous, and decomposition suggests the pooled result may substantially reflect differences between countries rather than a shared within-country mechanism.
  • Statistical significance is not the same as economic importance, and neither is the same as causality.
  • Economic size: in the log model, doubling digital-payment intensity is associated with a net fee margin about 0.71 percentage points of total assets lower (roughly 70 basis points), about 39% of the sample mean of 1.81%. Against the median (1.10%) the share is larger, so the percentage depends on the base.

The pooled negative association comes mainly from differences between countries — Indonesia averages a net fee margin of 0.022 at a digital intensity of 164% of GDP, Thailand 0.012 at 271%, Malaysia 0.012 at 540% — rather than from change within each country over time. That is the pattern contestable-markets theory predicts, because contestability is a property of market structure and entry conditions differ: Indonesia licenses e-money and peer-to-peer providers openly, Malaysia has issued digital-bank licences against mature incumbents, and Thailand's virtual-bank framework is led by incumbents — and it is in Thailand that the coefficient is weakly positive.

Limitations

What this study cannot claim

The results are conditional associations, not causal estimates. Eight constraints explain why.

  1. Interpretation: a coefficient describes an association under a model, not an identified causal effect.
  2. Data level and cluster counts: the explanatory measure varies only at the country-year level (about 27 distinct values across three countries and nine years), so the pooled estimate rests on few macro movements. Bank-level clustering (39 clusters) does not address cross-sectional correlation, which is why Driscoll–Kraay errors are also reported; country subsamples also have few clusters (9 Malaysian and 7 Thai banks).
  3. Coverage: three countries are included, so the study should not be generalised to ASEAN-5 or all banking markets.
  4. Omitted variables: regulation, payment infrastructure, macroeconomic conditions, and market concentration may affect both digital payments and fee margins.
  5. Income aggregation: the outcome is total non-interest income over assets, so it cannot separate payment, card, merchant-service or wealth-management fees, and it also contains trading and other non-interest revenue.
  6. Data access: the underlying bank panel cannot be redistributed, so the thesis regressions are inspectable but not publicly rerunnable.
  7. What the pressure measure counts: the IMF indicator records banks' own mobile and internet banking transactions, not activity on non-bank FinTech platforms, so it measures the digital intensity banks operate in rather than direct FinTech competition.
  8. Sample balance and timing: Indonesian banks supply 200 of the 344 observations (58%), weighting the pooled estimate toward that market, and reverse causality cannot be ruled out, although the lagged-regressor test reduces the concern.
Research agenda

What I would study next

The limitations above read as a research programme rather than a dead end. Four questions follow directly from them.

Identification

Separating within-country change from between-country structure

The decomposition is the starting point: the within-country coefficient is −0.0049 (p = 0.53) against a between-country −0.0124. The open question is what data would identify a within-country effect.

Heterogeneity

Which banks, and which business models

Size alone did not explain exposure: neither the Large Bank dummy nor its interaction was significant. Better measures of digital resilience, such as IT expenditure or the depth of platform and API partnerships, are the next step, alongside ownership and reliance on fee income.

Design

Designs that can identify a policy event

Staggered payment-licensing reforms are candidate events: Indonesia's e-money and peer-to-peer licensing, Malaysia's digital-bank licences (2020) and Thailand's virtual-bank framework (2024). A difference-in-differences or event-study design would first have to pass parallel-trends and treatment-definition tests, with inference suited to few clusters, such as a wild-cluster bootstrap.

Measurement

Finer revenue data than a single aggregate margin

Payments, cards, merchant services, and wealth management may not move together. Disaggregated fee income, and a payments measure closer to the bank than a country-year ratio, are the two changes most likely to sharpen the question.

This is the direction I am preparing to pursue in master's study. Nothing on this page claims it as a result of the completed project.

Next steps

Where the research should go next

  • Disaggregate non-interest income into payments, cards, merchant services, and wealth management.
  • Broaden country coverage to restore the ASEAN-5 design.
  • Move towards identification: staggered payment-licensing reforms as quasi-experiments, difference-in-differences or event-study designs, an external instrument for digital adoption, and dynamic panel methods. (A one-year lag of the regressor is already reported in the thesis.)
  • Strengthen inference with few clusters: a wild-cluster bootstrap now, and country-level clustering once more countries are added. (Driscoll–Kraay standard errors are already reported in the thesis.)
